Learn English useful for picking up prescription medicine. Practise phrases for filling a prescription, asking how to take medicine, understanding the instructions on a medicine label, and checking possible side effects and interactions with other medicines.

SBS Learn English will help you speak, understand and connect in Australia -  view all episodes.

This lesson is suitable for intermediate-level learners. After listening, test your knowledge with our quiz.

SKIP ADVERTISEMENT

Learning notes:

Learn everyday English for filling a prescription, understanding the instructions on a medicine label, and checking possible side effects and interactions with other medicines.

Examples of phrases you can use when filling a prescription:

  • I’d like to fill this prescription, please. 
  • I need to have this prescription filled. 
  • How long will it take to fill my prescription? 
  • I’d like to get this prescription, please. 

Examples of phrases you can use when asking how to take or use the medicine:

  • How often should I take it? 
  • How often should I use these eye drops? 
  • Should I take this once or twice a day? 
  • What time of day should I take it? 
  • Should I take it with food? 
  • Should I take this before or after food? 
  • Can I take this on an empty stomach? 

Examples of phrases you can use when asking about side effects and other medicines:

  • Will this medicine make me drowsy? 
  • Can I drive after taking this? 
  • Are there any side effects? 
  • Can I take it with my allergy medication? 
  • Can I take this with my other medication? 
  • Is it safe to take these medicines together? 

Colloquial expressions:

No worries is an informal way to say that something is fine or that you are happy to help.

Vocabulary:

A prescription is an instruction from an authorised health professional for a particular medicine.

A script is another word for prescription.

To fill a prescription means to take it to a pharmacy so the medicine can be prepared and supplied to you.

A pharmacy or chemist is a place where you can collect prescription medicines and buy other health products.

A pharmacist is the professional who prepares and supplies the medicine.

Medicine and medication mean the same, but medication is more formal.

How often is a phrase you can use to ask about how frequently or regularly something does or should happen.

A repeat means you can use the same prescription to get the medicine again. Your last repeat is the final time you can use that prescription.

To take something on an empty stomach means to eat or drink something when you have not recently eaten..

A label is the printed information on the medicine packet or bottle. It usually includes the patient’s name and directions for taking the medicine.

Twice a day means two times each day.

Drowsy means sleepy or less alert than usual.

A side effect is an extra, often unwanted effect of a medicine.

To take one medicine with another medicine means to use both medicines during the same period.

Learning notes:

When we want to talk about something in the future but we are not sure that it will happen, we can use a number of different ways. In the dialogue, we see how labels talk about extra effects that the a medication can have on some people. To do this, we use the modal verb ‘may’.

  • Claire: It may make you drowsy.

We use this when it is only possible – not necessarily certain – that something can happen. It is only a possibility, and not necessarily a very common one, or one that affects everyone who takes it.

Your prescription is ready. You reach the pharmacy counter to collect your medicine. Then the pharmacist asks a question, gives you instructions and mentions a side effect. Suddenly, picking up one small box of medicine feels like a very big English conversation.

Claire: Have you taken this medicine before?

Hi, I’m Kate, and in this episode, we’re practising everyday English you can use when picking up prescription medicine.

Prescription medicine must be prescribed by a doctor or another authorised health professional.

Other medicines can be bought without a prescription and are called over-the-counter medicines.

Now, let’s join Allan at the pharmacy as he picks up his prescription medicine and finds out how to take his medicine safely.

Allan: Hi, I’d like to fill this prescription, please.

Claire: No worries. Have you taken this medicine before?

Allan: No, I haven’t. How often should I take it, and should I take it with food?

Claire: The label says to take one tablet twice a day with food. It may make you drowsy.

Allan: Okay. Can I take it with my allergy medication?

In Australia, you may hear people use both the words pharmacy and chemist. Both refer to the place – usually a shop -where you can collect prescription medicines and buy other health products.

Pharmacists are healthcare professionals. In Australia, people sometimes also call them chemists.

So, let’s practise some of the phrases that can help you ask them clear questions and understand their instructions.

Allan first said,

I’d like to fill this prescription, please.

A prescription is an instruction from an authorised health professional for a particular medicine.

Another word that you will hear people use is ‘script’. You can only collect the medicine in a prescription or script from a licensed pharmacy.

To fill a prescription means to take it to a pharmacy who can then locate or prepare the medicine for you. You could say:

Can you please process this script?

Or:

Can you please fill this prescription?

You can also ask:

How many repeats are there left on this prescription?

A repeat means you can use the same prescription to get the medicine again.

You are on your last repeat.

Your last repeat is the final time you can use that prescription.

Claire then asked:

Have you taken this medicine before?

She is asking whether Allan has used the same medicine in the past. The pharmacist may ask this before explaining how to use it. You might answer:

Yes, I’ve taken it before.

Or:

No, this is my first time taking it.

You may hear either the word medicine and medication. They have similar meanings, but medication is a little more formal and can refer to a wide range of difference drugs that people take for their health

Allan then asked:

How often should I take it?

Allan wants to know how many times he should take the medicine and when. He uses the phrase ‘how often’ to ask about how regularly he needs to take it.

For example:

How often should I use these eye drops?

Or:

Should I take this once or twice a day?

You can also ask:

What time of day should I take it?

Allan also asked:

Should I take it with food?

Some medicines are taken with food, while others have different instructions. If you are not sure, you can ask the pharmacist. For example:

Should I take this before or after food?

Or:

Can I take this on an empty stomach?

To take something on an empty stomach means to take it when you have not recently eaten.

Claire replied:

The label says to take one tablet twice a day with food.

The label is the printed information attached to the medicine. It usually includes the patient’s name and directions for taking the medicine.

Twice a day means two times each day. You may also hear:

Take one tablet once a day.

Or you may need to follow a different instruction, such as:

Take two tablets three times a day.

The instructions will depend on the medicine, so always check your the label on the medicine yourself.

Claire then said:

It may make you drowsy.

Drowsy means sleepy or less alert than usual. You could ask:

Claire: Will this medicine make me drowsy?

Or:

Can I drive after taking this?

If something may make you drowsy, you probably shouldn't drive because you might not pay attention.

We use the word may when we want to say that something is possible, but it will not necessarily happen. So, It may make you drowsy means drowsiness is a possible side effect.

A side effect is an extra, often unwanted effect of a medicine. You could ask:

Are there any side effects?

Allan then asked an important question:

Can I take it with my allergy medication?

Here, take it with means use one medicine while also using another. You might ask:

Can I take this with my other medication?

Or:

Is it safe to take these medicines together?

We covered a lot so far, how about we have a break, hear the dialogue once again, and then we’ll practise.
